The organizing principle: leadership + culture + communication + resources + workplace design + standard processes → staff behavior and reliability → patient outcomes. Course material is primary; supplementary definitions are explicitly identified.

01Organizational factors

Six core factors

Leadership structure · communication norms · resource allocation · organizational culture · psychological safety · standardization/safety culture.

Culture

Shared values, norms, and practices → influence reporting, teamwork, innovation, and patient outcomes.

Psychological safety

Feeling safe to take interpersonal risks → speak up about errors, report concerns, and share ideas.

Leadership WalkRounds

Listen → log concerns → act → close the feedback loop. WalkRounds WITH feedback associated with ↑ safety culture and engagement, ↓ burnout, ↑ work–life balance.

Communication structures

Tiered huddles: frontline → unit → leadership. Boundary spanners connect frontline concerns to decision-makers.

Standard communication

SBAR, I-PASS, TeamSTEPPS = tools for consistent handoffs and teamwork.

Resilience pyramid

Individual coping/workarounds → team-level psychological safety and communication → organizational systemic improvement.

Safety continuum

Blame culture: individual fault/underreporting → neutral culture: limited proactive learning → learning culture: errors inform improvement.

02Workplace design and patient safety

Evidence-based design (EBD)

Apply research to lighting, layout, acoustics, and materials; include frontline staff; monitor outcomes afterward.

Single-patient rooms

↓ cross-contamination and noise; ↑ privacy, confidentiality, and sleep.

Decentralized nurse stations

Shorter walking distances + better sightlines → ↑ responsiveness and direct care time; ↓ fatigue and falls.

Layout example — lecture

Centralized: 8,362–9,422 steps/shift, 51 falls/3 months. Redesigned: mean 8,093 steps/shift, 23 falls/3 months (55% reduction). Observed example—not a universal effect size.

Daylight/windows

Associated with improved mood/alertness, less sleepiness, and fewer medication errors; lighting is a safety consideration.

Room placement

Bed, sink, nurse-station proximity, visibility, and privacy influence perceived quality and satisfaction.

Environmental stressors

Noise + clutter + poor lighting → interruptions, stress, errors; ergonomics → fewer staff injuries.

Infection-control design

HEPA filtration, airflow direction, ventilation, and sink placement support prevention of airborne/contact transmission and hand-hygiene compliance.

Falls

Unassisted transfers are a risk; clear sightlines and close stations improve access and observation.

03Safety culture and standardization

Safety culture

Shared values, beliefs, and norms prioritizing safety.

Standardization

Consistent use of evidence-based practices → ↓ unwarranted variation + more reliable implementation, measurement, and accountability.

Five culture components

Leadership commitment · teamwork/communication · just culture · learning culture · patient-centeredness.

Just culture

Encourages non-punitive error reporting and learning; distinct from ignoring accountability.

CUSP

Comprehensive Unit-Based Safety Program: combines technical and adaptive strategies; executive engagement, team training, evidence-based protocols.

CUSP flow

Identify safety issues → educate staff on safety science → engage executives → implement evidence-based interventions → measure/share outcomes.

Improvement tools

FMEA = prospective failure-mode analysis; RCA = retrospective root-cause analysis; checklists = standardized steps. [Brief explanations are contextual expansion.]

SPS collaborative

33 children’s hospitals used safety bundles + culture changes → 50% fewer serious safety events; hospital-acquired conditions fell by up to 71% (reported collaborative results).

Nursing leadership

Frontline leaders use safety surveys, SBAR, peer accountability, and empowerment to improve culture.

Exam trap: “Non-punitive” and “psychologically safe” do not mean abandoning standards or accountability. The target is reporting + learning + reliable follow-through, not silence or blame.

07RAPID-FIRE RECALL

Answer from memory, then open each item.

What is psychological safety?
Feeling safe to take interpersonal risks.
What changes WalkRounds from mere listening into a learning intervention?
Act on concerns and communicate feedback to frontline staff.
Name the six core organizational factors.
Leadership, communication, resources, culture, psychological safety, standardization/safety culture.
What is the tiered huddle order?
Frontline → unit → leadership.
Which station design showed a 55% reduction in falls in the lecture example?
Decentralized stations and workpoints with improved visibility.
What are three benefits of single-patient rooms?
Reduced cross-contamination, greater privacy, quieter sleep.
How do FMEA and RCA differ?
Prospective anticipation vs retrospective investigation.
Expand CUSP.
Comprehensive Unit-Based Safety Program.
What outcomes were reported for the SPS collaborative?
Serious safety events ↓ 50%; hospital-acquired conditions ↓ up to 71%.
Name four measurement categories in the final slide.
Falls/1,000 patient-days, HAIs, medication-error trend, staff burnout/safety culture scores (among others).
