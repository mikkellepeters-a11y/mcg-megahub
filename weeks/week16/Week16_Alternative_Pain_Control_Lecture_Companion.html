Complementary and integrative therapies for pain · Dr. Julie Dahl-Smith, DO

Lecture Companion · 52-slide course source

Learning objectives & big picture

  1. Discuss the role of complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) in pain management.
  2. Recognize how to discuss CAM approaches with patients.
  3. Cite reliable sources for CAM pain-management information.

Organizing principle: Pain may reflect ongoing nociception, altered central pain processing, or nerve damage. The lecture links chronic stress and sensitization to multimodal care, then introduces nutritional, psychological, physical, and acupuncture-related interventions.

MUST KNOW

Complementary = non-mainstream modality with conventional care; alternative = used instead of conventional care. Integrative medicine combines safe and effective approaches in whole-person care.

1 · What counts as CAM or integrative medicine?

The slide uses five NIH CAM categories: biologically based therapies (botanicals/probiotics), mind–body medicine (biofeedback, meditation, guided imagery), manual medicine (osteopathy, massage, chiropractic), energy medicine (e.g. Reiki/therapeutic touch), and whole medical systems.

Whole systems named in the slides include homeopathy, traditional Chinese medicine, Ayurveda, naturopathic medicine, as well as Unani, Siddha, Tibetan medicine, and Indigenous healing traditions. These are descriptions of systems, not endorsements of each intervention.

COMMON CONFUSION

An integrative approach should not substitute a poorly supported treatment for necessary evidence-based care. Evaluate each proposed intervention for efficacy, risk, interactions, and patient preference.

2 · How often do patients use CAM?

The lecture’s embedded NCCIH trend graphic reports 19.2% (2002) → 36.7% (2022) of U.S. adults using select complementary health approaches. The in-slide multiple-choice answer is rounded to 36%. This is a figure for select surveyed approaches, not every conceivable CAM treatment.

The breakdown distinguishes using an approach at all from using it specifically for pain. In 2022, the graph shows chiropractic care and acupuncture frequently being used for pain among their respective users; meditation and yoga also increased in overall use.

CLINICAL CONNECTION

Ask about supplements and outside treatments explicitly; patients may not classify them as medications. A neutral, curious question improves disclosure.

MSK CONNECTION

A patient with osteoarthritis and a patient with fibromyalgia can both describe widespread pain but may have different dominant mechanisms. Match care to mechanism, functional goals, and safety.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · What distinguishes nociplastic from nociceptive pain?

Nociplastic pain emphasizes altered amplification/processing in the nervous system, while nociceptive pain reflects activation of nociceptors by actual or threatened tissue injury.

4 · Stress response, inflammation, and pain sensitization

From threat to pain signaling

The lecture connects hypothalamic CRH/CRF and HPA-axis activation to cortisol, sympathetic responses, and inflammatory signaling. Tissue injury activates nociceptors and mediators; dorsal-horn terminals release substance P, glutamate, and CGRP, facilitating ascending pain information. The amygdala contributes salience, fear, and threat learning; the hippocampus contributes context and signals of safety.

With sustained activation, the lecture describes an amplifying loop: stress ↔ threat perception ↔ inflammatory and neural signaling. The lecture also discusses glucocorticoid resistance, changes in descending inhibition, neuroplasticity, and impacts on sleep, mood, metabolism, and immune responses.

Peripheral versus central sensitization

FOUNDATIONS LINK

Dorsal-horn glutamate and neuropeptide signaling links membrane excitation and synaptic plasticity to persistent clinical pain.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · A healed injury is still painful, and brushing clothing hurts. What mechanism and term fit?

Central sensitization may contribute; pain from an ordinarily non-painful stimulus is allodynia.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Why does a chronic pain treatment plan address sleep, emotion, activity, and cognition?

The lecture’s model treats ongoing pain as a network involving stress, neural processing, physical function, and inflammatory modulation—not only a tissue-level symptom.

5 · CAM modalities and nutrition for sensitization

The lecture lists CBT/pain reprocessing, acupuncture, exercise/aquatic therapy, mind–body interventions, osteopathic manipulation, massage, tai chi, and yoga as modalities discussed in relation to pain sensitization. The protective-factor graphic groups potentially supportive factors into psychological (self-efficacy/coping), lifestyle (activity, restorative sleep), social support, and physical capacity.

Mediterranean diet and fat types

The slides describe the Mediterranean diet as well studied and anti-inflammatory, citing associations with chronic disease reduction and osteoarthritis outcomes such as pain, stiffness, quality of life, and inflammatory/cartilage markers. Emphasize vegetables, fruit, legumes, whole grains, fish, and unsaturated fats over treating a dietary score as a standalone medical prescription.

MUFA: omega-9/oleic acid is highlighted. PUFA: the lecture contrasts omega-6-associated arachidonic acid pathways with omega-3-associated EPA/DHA pathways. The slide contains an internal terminology conflict: it labels GLA both anti-inflammatory (with evening primrose/borage/black currant) and inflammatory (with vegetable/corn oil); do not memorize those conflicting examples as a validated equivalence. Its intended point is to distinguish specific fatty acids and inflammatory mediator pathways rather than assume all fats in one class are identical.

EXAM TRAP

Question 2 asks whether one should recommend a higher omega-6:omega-3 ratio while following the Mediterranean pattern. The slide’s teaching direction is false; it emphasizes avoiding excessive omega-6 dominance.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · What makes a Mediterranean diet pattern different from a single anti-inflammatory supplement?

It is an overall eating pattern emphasizing multiple plant foods, fish, and unsaturated fats—not one ingredient isolated from context.

6 · Topicals, supplements, low-dose naltrexone, ketamine

The lecture lists topical diclofenac 1% (Voltaren) and topical lidocaine 4–5% among options, followed by turmeric/curcuminoids (with piperine), magnesium glycinate, and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM-e). These are heterogeneous therapies: topical prescriptions/OTC products, dietary supplements, and investigational/off-label approaches should not be conflated.

Quality cues named on slide 31 include GMP, NSF, USP and GRAS; these do not all indicate that a product has demonstrated clinical efficacy or is free of interactions.

BOARD / CLINICAL ENRICHMENT

Piperine can change drug exposure, and SAM-e may interact with serotonergic medications. During medication reconciliation, record specific product, dose, brand, and start date—not just “takes supplements”.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Why is “natural” not a reliable surrogate for “safe”?

Botanicals can vary in concentration and purity, cause adverse effects, and interact with prescription drugs.

7 · Manual, movement, and mind–body care

The lecture assigns its own evidence/level-of-approval ratings: exercise A, massage B, osteopathic manipulation A/B; for pain examples, yoga A/B, CBT B, meditation/breathing/guided imagery A, hypnosis/biofeedback B, tai chi/qigong A. These are the lecture’s ratings, not universally transferable grades for all pain diagnoses.

Osteopathic principles taught: body as a mind–body–spirit unit, self-regulation/healing, reciprocal relationship of structure and function, and rational treatment guided by these ideas. Chiropractic teaching focuses on spinal alignment, and the lecture contrasts the historical foundations.

The mindfulness meta-analysis summary reports benefits on pain severity, anxiety, and depression but not disability in the cited synthesis. Behavioral distress can improve without equivalent measured gains in function: ask about both outcomes separately.

CLINICAL CONNECTION

Set measurable goals: pain intensity, activity tolerance, sleep, mood, and function are related but nonidentical treatment outcomes.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · A patient reports less pain after mindfulness training but unchanged disability. Is that necessarily inconsistent with the lecture?

No. The lecture cites improved pain and emotional outcomes without a significant disability benefit in its reviewed synthesis.

8 · Sleep and treatment context

The sleep slide describes melatonin as a chronobiotic capable of shifting circadian timing and affecting sleep onset; it lists a dosing range and timing discussion. These are course descriptions rather than patient-specific dosing recommendations. Sleep hygiene and improved routines are a larger part of an integrative chronic pain plan.

For people with chronic pain, disrupted sleep can amplify sensitivity, distress, and poor function, while pain itself disrupts sleep. Addressing the bidirectional cycle may help symptoms even when structural pathology persists.

MSK CONNECTION

Combine adequate activity with recovery and sleep habits; an intervention may improve overall pain coping without directly changing joint morphology.

9 · Acupuncture: mechanism claims, practice, and variants

The lecture reviews acupuncture’s historical origins and licensing/certification structures, then highlights proposed peripheral neural and neuroimmune effects. Named mediators include β-endorphin, enkephalins, dynorphins, serotonin, norepinephrine, acetylcholine, dopamine, GABA, and substance P. These are proposed/associated pathways, not proof that every effect depends on each transmitter.

Course ratings: acupuncture A/B, safety A, and dry needling/trigger-point injection B. The lecture also depicts battlefield auricular acupuncture with specific external ear points and differentiates traditional tai chi (sequential martial-arts-derived movement) from qigong (often more accessible postures and breath-centered movement).

COMMON CONFUSION

Traditional concepts of qi or meridians and experimentally studied nerve/neuroimmune effects should be described as distinct explanatory frameworks.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · What is the difference between acupuncture and trigger-point dry needling in this lecture?

They are presented as different needling interventions with different stated evidence ratings; do not treat them as identical names for the same standardized protocol.

10 · How to discuss CAM with patients and where to check

Nonjudgmental clinical conversation

  1. Ask: “What nonprescription products or approaches have you tried for your pain?”
  2. Clarify goals: Is the patient seeking less pain, better sleep, mobility, or fewer medicines?
  3. Evaluate: Look at evidence for the specific condition, contraindications, provider training, interactions, cost, and whether conventional care is being replaced.
  4. Shared decision: Offer evidence-supported adjuncts when appropriate and revisit response and adverse effects.

Resources named by Professor Dahl-Smith

  • NIH NCCIH: research-based complementary-health topics, herbs, and patient/provider information
  • Cochrane complementary medicine reviews
  • MedlinePlus herbs and supplements
  • Memorial Sloan Kettering “About Herbs” database
  • VA Whole Health and UW–Madison Integrative Health
  • Integrative Medicine by Rakel; peer-reviewed integrative medicine journals; institutional resource libraries
  • NatMed, Epocrates, and professional acupuncture-accreditation/credentialing resources
MUST KNOW

A patient request for “natural pain control” is a starting point for a safety and evidence discussion—not an instruction to dismiss CAM or automatically substitute it for indicated conventional care.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Which source would you consult for a patient taking a botanical supplement with several prescriptions?

Use an evidence-based interaction/product resource such as NCCIH, MedlinePlus, Memorial Sloan Kettering About Herbs, or NatMed, cross-checked with the medication list.

MASTER THESE BEFORE MOVING ON

  1. CAM versus integrative care: adjunctive and substitutive use differ; safe integrative care combines appropriately evaluated therapies.
  2. Pain categories: nociceptive, nociplastic, and neuropathic mechanisms require different lines of reasoning.
  3. Sensitization: peripheral threshold lowering and central amplification help explain persistent pain, hyperalgesia, and allodynia.
  4. Evidence ≠ tradition: an intervention’s history, plausible mechanisms, symptom benefit, functional benefit, and safety require separate evaluation.
  5. Shared decisions: reconcile supplements, identify what the patient values, and use reliable resources.
THE MOST USEFUL FIRST AID CONNECTIONS

1. Pain physiology: dorsal-horn glutamatergic signaling connects afferent nociception to central processing.
2. NSAID topical diclofenac: COX/prostaglandin framework carries over from pharmacotherapy.
3. Neuropathic-pain agents: gabapentin/pregabalin are distinct from anti-inflammatory analgesics.
4. NMDA antagonism (ketamine) relates to excitatory central pain transmission, but efficacy varies by indication.
