Learning objectives & big picture

Explicit case objective (Winston): Apply anti-inflammatory pharmacotherapy to clinical scenarios. Barman’s slides do not list formal objectives; the following sequence tracks his lecture: PG biology → aspirin/NSAIDs → acetaminophen → DMARDs → targeted immunomodulators → anti-gout medications.

One organizing principle: First decide whether you need symptom suppression (PG-mediated pain or inflammation), disease modification (RA/immune cytokine pathways), or urate reduction (prevention of gout attacks). These are distinct endpoints even when the patient’s complaint is simply “joint pain.”

MUST KNOW

Symptom relief by NSAIDs is different from altering RA disease activity with DMARDs, and both differ from lowering the urate burden in chronic gout.

1 · Arachidonic acid, prostaglandins, and NSAIDs

Normal pathway → symptom → drug effect

Inflammation stimulates arachidonic acid metabolism. Cyclooxygenase (COX) activity supports prostaglandin and thromboxane production. Local PGE₂ and PGF₂α sensitize peripheral pain fibers by lowering the activation threshold; central prostaglandins contribute to pain and hypothalamic fever. Thus COX inhibition reduces prostaglandin-driven pain, fever, and inflammation.

HIGH YIELD

In Barman’s framing, COX-2 blockade is linked primarily to analgesia/anti-inflammation, whereas COX-1 blockade explains gastric mucosal toxicity and reduced platelet aggregation.

Aspirin: dose and irreversible platelet action

Platelets cannot replace inhibited COX by protein synthesis, so the slide notes an antiplatelet effect lasting 8–10 days. GI ulceration results from reduced protective PGs, while renal injury risk increases when kidneys depend on PG-mediated blood flow.

EXAM TRAP

Do not equate all NSAIDs with aspirin: aspirin’s platelet COX action is irreversible. The Barman lecture explicitly warns that ibuprofen coadministration can reduce aspirin’s antiplatelet COX effect.

Major harms and contraindications

GI erosion/bleeding; prolonged bleeding for aspirin; renal failure in patients with chronic kidney disease. The aspirin slides also mention hemophilia, pregnancy, renal disease, probenecid, and suspected viral infection in children, with Reye syndrome involving prominent brain and liver damage.

Salicylate poisoning: the Barman slide highlights sodium bicarbonate to alkalinize urine and increase salicylate excretion; the slide also lists gastric lavage. Management is context- and timing-dependent, so this is a teaching summary rather than a protocol.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Why can the same drug reduce knee pain yet worsen renal function?

It reduces inflammatory PG signaling at the joint but may also block renal prostaglandins needed to maintain perfusion.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · What differentiates aspirin from ibuprofen at the platelet?

Aspirin causes irreversible platelet COX inhibition, so TXA₂ suppression persists for the platelet lifespan; ibuprofen is reversible.

2 · Acetaminophen: useful analgesic, poor peripheral anti-inflammatory

Acetaminophen can reduce pain and fever but is not a strong treatment for inflamed joints. Barman specifically teaches its COX-related effect in a low-peroxide environment: brain and spinal cord have relatively low peroxide concentrations, while activated leukocytes generate high levels of peroxide at sites of inflammation.

Clinical consequence: it may help osteoarthritis pain or fever, but it does not substitute for an anti-inflammatory agent when the target problem is intense local inflammation.

COMMON CONFUSION

“Pain relief” does not imply “anti-inflammatory.” Acetaminophen differs from NSAIDs in the professor’s high-peroxide model.

The Winston handout emphasizes the risk of severe liver failure with overdose and gives N-acetylcysteine as the antidote. The Barman slides note short half-life (~2 hours), 30–60-minute peak concentration, and historical opioid/acetaminophen combinations.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Why is acetaminophen relatively ineffective against a highly inflamed synovium?

Local leukocyte-generated peroxides impair the drug’s COX-related activity; it works better in low-peroxide CNS tissue.

3 · DMARDs: alter the autoimmune disease process

Rheumatoid arthritis is immune-mediated, involving leukocyte function and cytokines such as interleukins and TNF-α. Unlike NSAIDs, DMARDs modify immune activity and therefore target the disease process instead of merely relieving pain.

Distinguish closely related names by their cellular target

Methotrexate: anti-inflammatory adenosine pathway. Leflunomide: nucleotide synthesis. Hydroxychloroquine: antigen processing via altered lysosomal pH. Cyclophosphamide: DNA damage. Cyclosporine: T-cell activation signaling.

FIRST AID CONNECTION

The Step 1 discriminator for methotrexate is its antifolate-related toxicity with folate rescue/supplementation, alongside adenosine-mediated anti-inflammatory effects. Distinguish it from leflunomide’s dihydroorotate dehydrogenase action.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Which DMARD causes immune suppression by reducing de novo pyrimidine synthesis?

Leflunomide, through dihydroorotate dehydrogenase inhibition.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Why can hydroxychloroquine impair antigen presentation?

It increases lysosomal pH, disrupting normal antigen processing.

4 · Target cytokines and their intracellular signaling

TNF-α activates inflammatory cells and pathways including NF-κB. Barman presents three TNF-blocking drugs that share a biological endpoint but differ structurally and by route:

Mechanism chain: cytokine ligand blockade prevents extracellular signaling (anti-TNF/anti-IL-17), while JAK blockade interrupts intracellular signal propagation. These interventions reduce pathologic inflammation but can impair host defenses.

FIRST AID CONNECTION

TNF signaling is important for maintaining granulomas. Anti-TNF treatment can reactivate latent tuberculosis; recognize this in a Step 1 vignette.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Which agent is NOT an antibody but instead a decoy receptor fused to IgG Fc?

Etanercept.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · How does tofacitinib differ from an anti-TNF antibody?

Tofacitinib inhibits intracellular JAK signaling; anti-TNF drugs bind extracellular cytokine or act as a receptor decoy.

5 · Gout pharmacotherapy: stop inflammation now, lower urate for prevention

Acute inflammatory attack

NSAIDs: reduce COX-dependent inflammatory PG synthesis; Winston’s MCQ singles out indomethacin. Colchicine: binds tubulin and impairs granulocyte movement, breaking the neutrophil-amplified inflammatory cycle. Barman labels it gout-specific and not itself a general analgesic. The handout notes early flare therapy as well as prophylaxis.

Colchicine adverse effects: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain; marrow suppression can occur due to antimitotic action. Acetaminophen and morphine do not control the inflammatory mechanism in the Winston decision slide. Low-dose aspirin can retain urate and is not the preferred acute gout drug.

Reduce uric acid production

Allopurinol: competitive inhibitor of xanthine oxidase; its metabolite oxypurinol also inhibits the enzyme. The pathway is hypoxanthine → xanthine → uric acid. Winston additionally lists febuxostat as an xanthine oxidase inhibitor. Initiating urate-lowering treatment can precipitate an acute flare; Barman teaches colchicine or indomethacin during initiation.

Increase uric acid excretion

Probenecid and sulfinpyrazone increase urate elimination by interfering with proximal-tubular urate reabsorption/transport as framed in the lecture. Maintain urine output; probenecid should not be used in patients with kidney stones. Winston also lists lesinurad and losartan in the uricosuric category; these are not identical in clinical role.

LECTURE → STEP 1 BRIDGE

Inflammation in a gout flare → target granulocyte function with colchicine; chronic urate accumulation → inhibit xanthine oxidase with allopurinol/febuxostat or increase urate excretion. This distinction drives drug-choice vignettes.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Why is allopurinol not simply another analgesic for an acute gout flare?

It decreases uric acid synthesis over time; it does not directly reverse the immediate inflammatory cascade.

6 · Winston clinical case: medication choice under comorbidity constraints

Case: 69-year-old with an acutely hot, swollen right knee, baseline OA and Heberden nodes, high BP, diabetes, GI symptoms and impaired renal function (creatinine 1.7 mg/dL). Inflammatory markers include WBC 12.4 × 10⁹/L and ESR 48 mm/h. The prompt proposes acute gout, RA, septic arthritis, pseudogout or trauma.

Three decisions in one vignette

  1. Diagnose the acute monoarthritis: the case teaching steers toward gout and uses a treatment question, but the acute hot joint also requires consideration of infection and crystal disease. The slides do not provide synovial fluid findings to establish one crystal diagnosis definitively.
  2. Choose an acute therapy: Winston’s answer slide selects indomethacin; its COX inhibition addresses inflammation. However, this patient has multiple NSAID risk factors.
  3. Prevent recurrence: on a second gout episode, Winston asks for the drug inhibiting xanthine oxidase. Answer: allopurinol.
SOURCE DISCREPANCY / CLINICAL SAFETY

Winston’s MCQ selects indomethacin for a gout flare, but the patient’s renal impairment, hypertension and GI symptoms make systemic NSAIDs higher-risk. The slides do not explicitly reconcile this risk. Clinically, suspected septic arthritis requires prompt evaluation, and acute-gout choice should be individualized rather than automatically using indomethacin.

BOARD / CLINICAL ENRICHMENT

When acute monoarthritis is accompanied by warmth, swelling and inflammatory markers, synovial fluid evaluation can help distinguish septic arthritis and gout/CPPD; imaging evidence of OA does not exclude infection.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Why does the knee X-ray not prove that the sudden acute attack is only OA?

It shows chronic degenerative changes, which can coexist with gout, CPPD or septic arthritis. The acute inflammatory presentation needs separate diagnostic reasoning.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Which drug in Winston’s prevention question inhibits xanthine oxidase?

Allopurinol.

MASTER THESE BEFORE MOVING ON

  1. COX physiology: know which prostaglandin effects explain analgesia, GI toxicity, bleeding, fever control and renal risk. This makes NSAID questions mechanistic rather than memorized.
  2. Symptom suppression versus disease modification: NSAIDs do not replace DMARDs in immune arthritis. This distinction carries through rheumatology treatment plans.
  3. Drug-target map: associate each DMARD or biologic with adenosine, pyrimidines, lysosomes, TNF, JAK or IL-17. These are frequent look-alike answer choices.
  4. Acute gout versus prevention: inhibit inflammation now; decrease total urate load over time. Separate colchicine, indomethacin and allopurinol mechanisms.
  5. Comorbidity-driven decisions: renal impairment and GI disease change NSAID risk even when the lecture’s board-style choice is an NSAID.

The most useful First Aid connections

1. Aspirin’s irreversible platelet COX-1 effect. 2. NSAID-associated AKI risk when renal perfusion depends on prostaglandins. 3. MTX folate-associated toxicity alongside adenosine. 4. Anti-TNF and TB reactivation. 5. Allopurinol’s xanthine oxidase interaction with azathioprine (outside lecture, labeled First Aid bridge).
