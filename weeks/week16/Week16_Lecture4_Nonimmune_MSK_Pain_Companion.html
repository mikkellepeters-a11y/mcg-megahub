Two organizing questions: Is the patient’s problem a weak connective-tissue scaffold, or altered pain processing? If pain dominates, is it generalized and centrally amplified (fibromyalgia), or regional after injury with autonomic/trophic changes (CRPS)?

Objective 1 · Ehlers–Danlos syndromes (EDS)

WHAT: Heritable disorders that alter collagen production or modification, impairing tissue tensile support. Clinical consequences depend on which collagen-rich tissues are affected: ligamentous laxity → hypermobility; skin involvement → hyperextensibility or easy bruising; arterial/visceral weakness → rupture risk. Thirteen subtypes are recognized in the lecture; focus on the following three.

MUST KNOW

Atrophic tissue-paper scarring + marked hypermobility points to classical EDS (type V collagen). Translucent skin + spontaneous arterial/organ rupture points to vascular EDS (type III collagen). Hypermobile EDS has pronounced laxity without the classical papyraceous scars.

Why lax connective tissue causes mechanical pain

Weak stabilizing support permits excessive joint excursion → recurrent strain or dislocation → activity-related pain, often worse later in the day. This contrasts with an inflammatory pattern characterized by synovitis and prolonged morning stiffness. Generalized hypermobility may decrease with age and can be associated with premature osteoarthritis.

Beighton scoring: The lecture depicts the 9-point assessment and states that a current or historical score ≥5 supports hypermobility. Components include bilateral fifth-finger extension, thumb-to-forearm apposition, elbow and knee hyperextension, and forward trunk flexion with palms to floor. The score describes mobility; it is not by itself an EDS diagnosis.

Vascular EDS: the dangerous phenotype

Type III collagen supports reticulin in blood vessels and hollow viscera. Reduced integrity of these tissues explains aneurysm, dissection or rupture and bowel/uterine complications. Unlike classical EDS, severe skin stretching and marked generalized joint laxity are not required.

CLINICAL CONNECTION

In a patient with aneurysm or spontaneous visceral rupture plus translucent skin and unusual bruising, connect the vascular-wall fragility to a COL3A1/type III collagen defect.

Management logic

Diagnosis is phenotype-based with subtype-appropriate genetic testing (the lecture emphasizes absence of an identified mutation in hypermobile EDS). Physical/occupational therapy, joint protection, orthoses and task modification limit repeated injury. Vascular imaging surveillance or preconception counseling is individualized by subtype.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · A patient dislocates shoulders repeatedly and has broad, thin “cigarette-paper” scars. Which collagen type is defective?
Type V collagen (COL5A1/COL5A2), classical EDS. The scar morphology separates this from hypermobile EDS.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Why is vascular EDS especially dangerous even with minimal hypermobility?
Type III collagen weakness impairs artery and hollow-organ integrity, creating rupture/dissection risk despite a less dramatic joint phenotype.

Objective 1 · Marfan syndrome

WHAT: An autosomal-dominant, pleiotropic connective-tissue condition caused by an FBN1 mutation on chromosome 15, affecting the extracellular glycoprotein fibrillin-1.

HOW: Fibrillin-1 bundles support zonular fibers and elastic-fiber scaffolds and help restrain TGF-β. Deficiency causes both reduced tissue integrity/elasticity and excess growth signaling. This single mechanism makes sense of ocular, cardiovascular and skeletal findings.

MECHANISM CHAIN

FBN1 mutation → defective fibrillin microfibrils + dysregulated TGF-β → weakened elastic tissues and overgrowth → aortic root dilation, ectopia lentis, skeletal overgrowth.

SystemLecture findingsMechanistic connection
SkeletalTall stature, long limbs/short torso, arm span:height >1.05, arachnodactyly, pectus excavatum, scoliosis, high palate, hypermobilityAltered matrix support and growth regulation
OcularEctopia lentis, usually upward; retinal detachmentWeak lens zonular fibers

EXAM TRAP

Marfan syndrome has a fibrillin defect, not a collagen V or III defect. Aortic root disease and lens dislocation are the characteristic multi-organ pairing.

FIRST AID CONNECTION

First Aid 2025 (lens disorders, PDF p. 571) also links ectopia lentis to homocystinuria. The lecture identifies upward lens displacement in Marfan; distinguishing the etiologies can be tested in ocular vignettes.

Management in the slides: genetic confirmation; annual ophthalmology; serial aortic imaging; beta-blockers when indicated to slow aortic root enlargement; PT/OT; avoid contact sports, exertion to exhaustion and Valsalva-heavy isometrics that can precipitate dissection.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · A tall patient with long fingers develops aortic root dilation. Why can a fibrillin defect affect both the aorta and lens?
Fibrillin-1 forms microfibrils in elastic artery support and ocular zonular fibers, so one FBN1 defect disrupts both tissues.

Objective 1 · Osteogenesis imperfecta: objective gap

SOURCE NOTE

The official learning objective includes osteogenesis imperfecta, but the supplied slides do not teach its genetics, phenotype or management. The following narrow bridge is taken from the uploaded First Aid 2025 reference, not from the lecture.

FIRST AID CONNECTION

COL1A1 or COL1A2 mutation → abnormal type I collagen → impaired strong bone matrix and brittle bones. First Aid’s rapid-review table specifically connects the genes to defective type I collagen triple-helix formation (PDF p. 729).

BOARD / CLINICAL ENRICHMENT: Classically consider recurrent low-trauma fractures, blue sclerae, hearing impairment and dentinogenesis imperfecta. These associations are a study bridge for the stated objective; their detailed phenotype is not presented in the lecture slides.

DON’T MIX THESE UP

OI = type I collagen and fragile bone; classical EDS = type V collagen and papyraceous scars; vascular EDS = type III collagen and rupture; Marfan = fibrillin-1 and aortic/lens features.

Objective 2 · Fibromyalgia

WHAT: A chronic, noninflammatory widespread pain syndrome. The lecture describes worldwide prevalence of 2–3% (higher in the US), female predominance and a peak onset at ages 30–55. Most important are widespread pain, fatigue and disrupted/nonrestorative sleep.

WHY/HOW: Central sensitization means CNS pain-processing networks respond excessively to ordinary or normally nonpainful input. Allodynia is pain from a stimulus that does not normally produce pain. In the lecture’s model, pain can become persistently amplified without ongoing peripheral tissue inflammation: nociplastic pain.

DomainCharacteristic pattern
PainDiffuse or “hurts all over,” aching/burning/stabbing; tenderness at articular and non-articular sites; allodynia

Potential triggers and associated conditions

Early-life or acute trauma (including whiplash), infections, obstructive sleep apnea, inflammatory arthritis (including RA or SLE), PTSD/catastrophic events and prolonged psychological stress appear in the lecture. These are associations or precipitants, not proof of a single cause.

EXAM TRAP

Normal inflammatory markers and normal imaging support a noninflammatory pain syndrome in the right clinical context. A patient can have both inflammatory arthritis and coexisting fibromyalgia; do not assume that one automatically excludes the other.

Treatment follows the central-pain model

First-line: education that validates symptoms and promotes self-management, graded aerobic activity/fitness, attention to sleep, and cognitive behavioral therapy if accessible. Tai chi, yoga, massage and acupuncture are complementary approaches discussed in the slides. Second-line medications: neuromodulators such as duloxetine, pregabalin and milnacipran. The lecturer’s summary advises against opioids or NSAIDs as fibromyalgia treatments.

FIRST AID CONNECTION

First Aid 2025 (PDF p. 498) reinforces the clustering of diffuse pain, tender points, nonrestorative sleep, cognitive disturbance and normal ESR; it also mentions TCAs/SNRIs and gabapentinoid agents as pharmacologic categories.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Why can a fibromyalgia patient have severe diffuse pain with a normal ESR and no synovitis?
The pain is centrally amplified (nociplastic), rather than a direct readout of ongoing inflammatory joint injury.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · What two treatment categories are front-line before adding neuromodulators?
Education/self-management and graded exercise; CBT and sleep optimization are additional central first-line strategies in the lecture.

Objective 3 · Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS)

WHAT: A regional pain syndrome arising typically in one extremity after trauma or surgery, featuring disproportionate pain, allodynia/hyperalgesia and autonomic vasomotor abnormalities. The lecture reports a clear inciting event in ~75%, often fracture, sprain or surgery, with onset within three months in ~80%.

MECHANISM CHAIN

Injury → sensory pain impulse → sympathetic activation at injury site → vasomotor/inflammatory response → swelling, temperature/color change and disproportionate pain. This is the lecture’s reflex sympathetic dystrophy explanatory model; pathophysiology is more complex than a single exclusive pathway.

Recognition: Burning pain, red or blue discoloration, edema, temperature instability (often cool), hyperhidrosis and restricted range of motion spread beyond the originally injured joint or site. The combination of pain plus regional autonomic changes distinguishes CRPS from isolated arthritis.

HIGH YIELD

Trauma + persistent regional burning pain + vasomotor/sudomotor changes is more informative than pain severity alone. Late patchy osteopenia is a CRPS complication, not evidence by itself of systemic inflammatory arthritis.

Treatment principles taught

Multidisciplinary management: PT, CBT, IV ketamine, calcitonin/bisphosphonates and selected spinal-cord stimulation. The slide notes that high-dose glucocorticoids may be considered only early (<3–6 months); it describes a specific historical regimen, not an instruction for self-treatment. The lecturer reports about half of selected stimulation recipients obtaining ~50% reduction in pain.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Following wrist surgery, a patient has burning hand pain, sweating changes, blue color and swelling beyond the wrist. Which syndrome, and why not simple inflammatory arthritis?
CRPS: a post-traumatic regional pain syndrome with autonomic and sensory abnormalities extending beyond a synovial joint.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Which late-stage X-ray finding is presented as a CRPS complication?
Patchy osteoporosis/osteopenia due to regional demineralization.

Master these before moving on

  1. Collagen subtype → tissue phenotype. Type V explains classical EDS skin/scar and laxity; type III explains vascular-wall and organ rupture. This is the foundation for differentiating the EDS subtypes.
  2. Fibrillin versus collagen. Marfan affects microfibrils and TGF-β regulation, connecting long-bone proportions with lens and aortic changes.
  3. Central sensitization. Fibromyalgia does not require detectable inflammatory destruction to generate widespread pain; symptoms and normal tests fit together.
  4. Region + autonomic changes. CRPS arises after injury and causes temperature, sweating and color changes beyond a joint; recognize its evolving imaging/skin findings.

The most useful First Aid connections

  1. OI: COL1A1/COL1A2 → type I collagen defect (First Aid 2025, PDF p. 729; outside lecture).
  2. Fibromyalgia: diffuse pain + poor sleep/brain fog + normal ESR (p. 498).
  3. Marfan: ectopia lentis also requires distinguishing homocystinuria (p. 571).
  4. EDS: systemic collagen weakness connects skin and articular laxity (p. 729).
LECTURE → STEP 1 BRIDGES

(1) Paper-like scars + hypermobility → COL5A1/2. (2) Translucent skin + spontaneous arterial rupture → COL3A1. (3) Marfanoid body + lens dislocation + aortic disease → FBN1. (4) Diffuse pain + non-restorative sleep + normal inflammatory markers → fibromyalgia. (5) Post-traumatic burning limb + skin temperature/sweat change → CRPS.
