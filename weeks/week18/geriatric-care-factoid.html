Patient-Centered Care, Communication & Shared Decision-Making in Older Adult Care · Factoid Sheet

Rapid-review · Definition → mechanism → outcome · Jirau-Rosaly

One-minute framework

Accessible communication → patient values → capacity → shared decision → safe-enough plan → reassess. Family supports the patient rather than displacing them.

Objectives 1–2 · Communication

HearingCheck hearing aids and earwax; reduce noise; speak loudly and clearly in a normal tone; face at eye level; signal topic changes; use notepad or amplification.
VisionAdequate light, eyewear, handwritten instructions if readable, audio instructions, large pictures and aids; clarify understanding.
CognitionKnow the person; orient; use brief, direct sentences, examples and analogies; rephrase rather than quiz memory.
Voice/speech, language, culture, environmentRecognize these as communication barriers; technology, noise and darkness can also interfere.
MUST KNOW

Fellow adult; comfort; unhurried; plain language; face-to-face; print/write takeaways; protect privacy and ask the patient directly.

Objective 3 · Independence

Loss of functionPain, memory changes, reduced activity → altered self-image and identity.
Loss of independenceReduced roles and social activity → distress or isolation; explore what matters.

Objective 4 · Capacity and dignity of risk

EXAM TRAP

Capacity = clinical, decision-specific, may fluctuate. Competency = legal, court. Age, dementia and disagreement alone ≠ incapacity.

ComponentTestExample
UnderstandingExplain relevant factsWhat do you understand about your family’s driving concerns?
AppreciationApply the facts to oneselfWhat could happen if you got lost while driving?
ReasoningCompare options and justify preferenceWhat are the pros and cons of continuing to drive?
Communication of a choiceConsistently express a decisionWhat would you choose?

Dignity of risk · five elements

  • Clarify older adults’ values and goals
  • Assess and support decision-making capacity
  • Distinguish everyday life risk from high, non-negotiable risk
  • Weigh psychological benefits alongside physical risks
  • Document “safe enough” risk-sharing plans

Objective 6 · Advance care planning

WhenAny adult age/stage; not just end of life.
ProcessShare values, goals and future preferences; revisit, not one-and-done.
ResourcesConversation Project · PREPARE · POLST.

Objective 7 · Care approaches

Person-centeredFunctional goals, home/work responsibilities, symptoms, worries, available support and recovery goals.
Relationship-centeredRapport; coordinate therapy, nursing, hospital medicine, social work, primary care and community resources.
Person- and family-centeredIdentify all caregivers, their abilities and willingness, family preferences and ways to integrate them.
Caregiver-centeredCaregiver health, quality of life, willingness, ability and effects of illness/discharge on the caregiver.
HIGH YIELD

All four approaches coexist; caregiver willingness and functional ability are crucial for discharge.

First Aid rapid connections

Capacity → informed consentCapacity is only one component; disclosure, understanding and voluntariness also matter. A signed form alone is insufficient. (p. 268)
Autonomy → refusalAn informed patient can decline care despite clinician preference. Beneficence and nonmaleficence do not automatically override autonomy. (p. 267)
Advance directives → surrogateA living will records preferences; a medical power of attorney designates an agent for loss of capacity. A surrogate should reflect the patient’s wishes when known; legal details vary. (pp. 268–269)
Disability → direct communicationAsk preferred communication method; speak to the patient; give extra time and reduce distractions. Use a trained interpreter when language access requires it. (p. 274)
DNR → CPR onlyA DNR does not automatically rule out other treatments. (p. 268)

Rapid-fire recall

1. An older patient’s daughter answers every question. What should you do?

Direct questions to the patient, protect privacy, and involve the daughter with the patient’s permission.

2. A patient cannot hear the discussion. Does this establish incapacity?

No. Address hearing barriers first and reassess the actual decision-making abilities.

3. What distinguishes understanding from appreciation?

Understanding is explaining the facts; appreciation is recognizing their personal relevance.

4. Does a dementia diagnosis remove capacity?

No. Capacity is clinical, decision-specific and may fluctuate.

5. Why may stopping driving be psychologically harmful?

Driving may support identity, responsibilities, social participation and independence; losing it can cause isolation or distress.

6. What are the five elements of dignity-of-risk negotiation?

Clarify values; support capacity; distinguish risk levels; weigh benefits and harms; document a safe-enough plan.

7. Expand SHARE.

Seek participation; Help compare options; Assess values; Reach a decision; Evaluate the decision.

8. When should advance care planning occur?

At any adult age or health stage, with revisiting over time.

9. Which advance directive names a decision-maker?

Medical power of attorney (First Aid p. 268).

10. Does DNR mean no other treatment?

No. It limits CPR, not every other intervention (First Aid p. 268).
