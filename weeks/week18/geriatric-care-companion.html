Patient-Centered Care, Communication & Shared Decision-Making in Older Adult Care

Lecture Companion · Learning objectives, explanations, clinical applications and retrieval checkpoints · Jirau-Rosaly

Learning Objectives

  1. Identify effective communication strategies with older adults and adult children while maintaining autonomy.
  2. Adapt communication for cognitive impairment.
  3. Recognize psychosocial effects of loss of independence.
  4. Balance autonomy, safety, and family concerns.
  5. Apply shared decision-making principles.
  6. Discuss advance directives and advance care planning.
  7. Use patient- and family-centered care in common geriatric scenarios.

Big Picture

The lecture frames geriatric care as a partnership rather than a safety-only exercise. Older adults frequently have multiple chronic conditions; family, caregivers and interprofessional teams often participate in treatment. The lecture reports that one in six Americans is at least 65. Communication failures contribute to dissatisfaction and medical error.

CORE REASONING MODEL

Remove communication barriers → understand the person’s goals → assess decision-specific capacity when needed → compare options and risk → agree on a plan → reassess.

Objective 1 · Communication and autonomy

Address the older adult as a fellow adult, not as a diagnosis or dependent child. Create comfort, avoid rushing, use plain language, face the patient, and write or print key takeaways. Invite a previsit concern list when helpful. Include caregivers without turning the interview into a conversation about the patient rather than with the patient.

HearingCheck hearing aids and earwax; reduce noise; speak loudly and clearly in a normal tone; face at eye level; signal topic changes; use notepad or amplification.
VisionAdequate light, eyewear, handwritten instructions if readable, audio instructions, large pictures and aids; clarify understanding.
CognitionKnow the person; orient; use brief, direct sentences, examples and analogies; rephrase rather than quiz memory.
Voice/speech, language, culture, environmentRecognize these as communication barriers; technology, noise and darkness can also interfere.
MUST KNOW

Always direct questions to the patient; involve family while protecting privacy and honoring preferences.

FIRST AID CONNECTION

Ask preferred communication method; speak to the patient; give extra time and reduce distractions. Use a trained interpreter when language access requires it. (p. 274)

An older patient’s daughter answers every question. What should you do?

Direct questions to the patient, protect privacy, and involve the daughter with the patient’s permission.

Objective 2 · Adapt communication for cognitive impairment

Know the person, not simply the diagnosis. Orient the patient to the setting and purpose of the visit. Short sentences and concrete examples reduce the demand on memory and processing. If the patient does not understand, change how the information is presented rather than repeating it impatiently or testing recall with “remember when” or “as I said.”

EXAM TRAP

Communication difficulty is not proof of impaired decision-making. Optimize hearing, vision and language access before judging capacity.

A patient cannot hear the discussion. Does this establish incapacity?

No. Address hearing barriers first and reassess the actual decision-making abilities.

Objective 3 · Psychosocial effects of loss of independence

The closing poem emphasizes that physical decline, memory difficulties, pain and inability to do familiar activities may change someone’s self-image without changing their inherent worth. Loss of independence can threaten identity, social participation, roles and emotional well-being. A patient who resists help may be protecting a valued part of their life, not simply being noncompliant.

CLINICAL CONNECTION · illustrative application

A patient who no longer drives may lose access to friends, worship, shopping or appointments. Explore what driving enables before proposing alternatives. The aim is to preserve meaningful activity while addressing risk.

Why may stopping driving be psychologically harmful?

Driving may support identity, responsibilities, social participation and independence; losing it can cause isolation or distress.

Objective 4 · Balance autonomy, safety and family concerns

Autonomy is the right to self-determination and informed decisions. Competency is a legal determination by a court; capacity is a clinical determination assessed by clinicians, specific to a decision and potentially fluctuating. Age, dementia or disagreement with the clinical team does not automatically remove capacity.

Capacity componentWhat it testsDriving example (adapted from slide 12)
UnderstandingExplain relevant factsWhat do you understand about your family’s driving concerns?
AppreciationApply the facts to oneselfWhat could happen if you got lost while driving?

Communication of a choiceConsistently express a decisionWhat would you choose?
COMMON CONFUSION

A patient may understand that driving is risky yet fail to appreciate that those risks apply personally. Conversely, accepting risk after understanding and reasoning is not itself incapacity.

FIRST AID CONNECTION

Capacity is only one component; disclosure, understanding and voluntariness also matter. A signed form alone is insufficient. (p. 268) An informed patient can decline care despite clinician preference. Beneficence and nonmaleficence do not automatically override autonomy. (p. 267)

Dignity of risk

Meaningful living and identity can depend on the freedom to accept uncertainty or potential harm. Negotiation respects preferences while discussing safety concerns, rather than attempting to eliminate every risk.

  1. Clarify older adults’ values and goals
  2. Assess and support decision-making capacity
  3. Distinguish everyday life risk from high, non-negotiable risk
  4. Weigh psychological benefits alongside physical risks
  5. Document “safe enough” risk-sharing plans
CLINICAL CONNECTION · illustrative application

After a fall, a patient values cooking independently. Explore what cooking means, evaluate capacity and specific hazards, negotiate practical help or modifications and document the agreed safety plan. Risks to others or serious hazards require separate attention.

What distinguishes understanding from appreciation?

Understanding is explaining the facts; appreciation is recognizing their personal relevance.

What are the five elements of dignity-of-risk negotiation?

Clarify values; support capacity; distinguish risk levels; weigh benefits and harms; document a safe-enough plan.

Objective 5 · Shared decision-making

Shared decision-making combines the clinician’s knowledge of available options with the patient’s values. It is not simply obtaining permission for a predetermined plan or leaving the patient without guidance. The AHRQ SHARE model in the lecture organizes the interaction.

S · SeekInvite patient participation
H · HelpExplore and compare treatment options
A · AssessElicit patient values and preferences
R · ReachArrive at a decision with the patient
E · EvaluateReview the patient’s decision
Expand SHARE.

Seek participation; Help compare options; Assess values; Reach a decision; Evaluate the decision.

Objective 6 · Advance directives and advance care planning

Advance care planning supports adults at any age or health stage in understanding and sharing personal values, life goals and preferences for future medical care. It is not restricted to the end of life and is not a one-time task. Revisit preferences as circumstances change.

  • Lecture resources: The Conversation Project, PREPARE, and POLST.
  • Ask what outcomes matter, who should participate, and what future care aligns with the patient’s goals.
FIRST AID CONNECTION

A living will records preferences; a medical power of attorney designates an agent for loss of capacity. A surrogate should reflect the patient’s wishes when known; legal details vary. (pp. 268–269) A DNR does not automatically rule out other treatments. (p. 268)

BOARD / CLINICAL ENRICHMENT

Advance care planning is a conversation process; an advance directive records wishes or designates a decision-maker. POLST is a portable medical-order approach for appropriate seriously ill or frail patients; local implementation varies.

When should advance care planning occur?

At any adult age or health stage, with revisiting over time.

Objective 7 · Patient- and family-centered scenarios

The lecture’s four approaches are complementary, not mutually exclusive. Start with what the patient wants to achieve, then consider the relationships and resources that make the plan achievable.

Person-centeredFunctional goals, home/work responsibilities, symptoms, worries, available support and recovery goals.
Relationship-centeredRapport; coordinate therapy, nursing, hospital medicine, social work, primary care and community resources.
Person- and family-centeredIdentify all caregivers, their abilities and willingness, family preferences and ways to integrate them.
Caregiver-centeredCaregiver health, quality of life, willingness, ability and effects of illness/discharge on the caregiver.

Master these before moving on

  • Capacity is not competency. Decision-specific clinical capacity is not a global legal judgment.
  • Accessibility precedes assessment. A communication barrier can obscure abilities.
  • Autonomy includes meaningful risk. Negotiate safety without dismissing patient identity.
  • SHARE is a process. Patient values drive a joint decision and later evaluation.
  • Advance care planning is ongoing. Preferences and circumstances may change.
