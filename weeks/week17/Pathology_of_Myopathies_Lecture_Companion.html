BIG PICTURE

Weakness is a localization problem before it is a disease-name problem. A failed motor neuron or axon causes neurogenic atrophy; failed neuromuscular transmission produces junctional weakness; disease of the myofiber itself produces a primary myopathy. Once localized, the pattern of weakness, CK, EMG and biopsy morphology separate dystrophy, mitochondrial disease, inflammatory disease, selective type-2 atrophy and denervation.

Official lecture objectives

  1. Review skeletal muscle histology: the four components of a motor unit and type I/type II muscle fibers.
  2. Outline clinical and laboratory assessment of muscle disorders and the diagnostic approach to muscle biopsy.
  3. Recognize pathophysiology, clinical/pathologic findings, diagnosis, differential diagnosis and outcomes of Duchenne and Becker dystrophies, mitochondrial myopathy, polymyositis/dermatomyositis/inclusion body myositis, type-2 fiber atrophy and neurogenic atrophy (ALS, SMA, diabetic neuropathy).

1 · Normal motor unit and muscle histology

MUST KNOW
Four motor-unit components: motor neuron cell body → motor axon → neuromuscular junction → muscle fiber. Match the lesion to the component. Motor neuron/axon: neurogenic atrophy; NMJ: myasthenia gravis; muscle fiber: myopathy.

Normal cross-sections show relatively uniform polygonal/rounded fibers with peripheral nuclei. The endomysium surrounds individual fibers, perimysium surrounds fascicles and epimysium surrounds the whole muscle. These planes later explain why endomysial and perimysial/perivascular inflammatory patterns matter.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Why would selective type-II fiber atrophy be recognizable on ATPase staining?

ATPase fiber typing distinguishes type I from type II; a selective reduction in type-II fiber caliber signals a patterned process rather than random fiber loss.

2 · Diagnostic approach: bedside → laboratory → biopsy

Presentation: delayed milestones or abnormal gait in children; sports difficulties in adolescents; chair-rise, stairs or overhead-arm difficulty in adults. Proximal weakness supports a myopathic process, while distal atrophy and sensory abnormalities favor neuropathy; these are guides, not absolute rules. Myalgia/tenderness suggests myositis or rhabdomyolysis. Myotonia is delayed relaxation after muscle use.

Tests: CK/CPK often rises with muscle-fiber injury (myositis and early muscular dystrophy). Myositis autoantibodies include anti-Jo-1 and anti-SRP. Blood glucose/HbA1c evaluate diabetic neuropathy. EMG helps distinguish myopathic from neurogenic units; MRI T2 fat-suppressed/STIR hyperintensity is a course-listed inflammatory myopathy clue.

COMMON CONFUSION
Degeneration in this lecture: pale cytoplasm, necrosis/fragmentation and macrophage clearance. Regeneration: more basophilic/purplish cytoplasm, enlarged nuclei and prominent nucleoli. Neither finding by itself identifies a unique disease.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · An H&E section has small angular fibers; is the primary lesion more likely myopathic or neurogenic?

Neurogenic. The abrupt angular atrophy reflects loss of neural input; reinnervation may later lead to fiber-type grouping.

3 · Duchenne versus Becker muscular dystrophy

Normal dystrophin couples the contractile apparatus to the sarcolemma and extracellular matrix, providing mechanical stability during contraction and signaling interactions. Loss of this linkage permits contraction-related membrane tears, Ca²⁺ influx → protease activation and mitochondrial dysfunction → myofiber necrosis. When degeneration exceeds regeneration, fibers are gradually replaced by fibrosis and adipose tissue.

Dystrophin defect → sarcolemmal fragility → membrane tears → ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺ → myofiber degeneration → fibrosis/fat replacement + high serum CK

Clinical progression (lecture): early pelvic-girdle weakness and motor delay → difficulty rising/climbing stairs → waddling gait and calf pseudohypertrophy → contractures, loss of ambulation, scoliosis → respiratory weakness and dilated cardiomyopathy. Gower maneuver is using the hands to climb up the thighs when standing because proximal hip extensors are weak.

Diagnosis: serum CK, then blood-based molecular testing (next-generation sequencing as taught) for confirmation and counseling. Biopsy remains useful if genetics are inconclusive or discordant or when an inflammatory myopathy is an alternative. H&E shows variable fiber diameters, rounded fibers, internal nuclei, necrosis/regeneration and eventual fibro-adipose replacement.

FIRST AID CONNECTION
DMD and BMD are allellic dystrophinopathies: the classic reading-frame explanation helps predict whether functional dystrophin is absent or partly retained. Muscle weakness plus elevated CK supports muscle membrane injury rather than a pure junctional transmission defect.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · A boy has proximal weakness, Gower maneuver, markedly elevated CK and absent dystrophin staining. What process produces his weakness?

Duchenne dystrophy: loss of dystrophin destabilizes the membrane, causing contraction-induced muscle-fiber necrosis with eventual fibrofatty replacement.

4 · Mitochondrial myopathy

Defective oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) limits ATP production, particularly injuring energy-dependent tissues such as skeletal muscle and nervous system. Thus exercise intolerance, early fatigue, myalgia, proximal weakness and ophthalmoplegia may coexist with seizures, stroke-like episodes, hearing loss, neuropathy or ataxia.

The lecture lists maternally inherited mtDNA disorders (MELAS, MERRF, Kearns–Sayre, chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia) and nuclear-DNA disorders including Leigh syndrome. MELAS connects myopathy, lactic acidosis and stroke-like episodes; MERRF connects myoclonus/epilepsy and ragged-red fibers.

Recognition: mild CK elevation, lactic acidosis, possible FGF-21 elevation; ragged-red fibers on modified Gomori trichrome due to abnormal subsarcolemmal mitochondrial accumulation. Electron microscopy on the original slide shows abnormal mitochondrial cristae/crystals. Genetic testing can be complicated by heterogeneous mutations.

MSK CONNECTION
Type I fibers normally have high mitochondrial density (slide 6). Mitochondrial disease causes failure of energy generation, not simply a lower percentage of type I fibers.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Exercise intolerance, ophthalmoplegia, lactic acidosis and ragged-red fibers point to failure of what process?

Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and ATP production.

5 · Inflammatory myopathies: recognize location and morphology

Dr. Sharma’s major autoimmune entities are polymyositis (PM), dermatomyositis (DM) and inclusion body myositis (IBM). They are separated by muscle distribution, skin findings, biopsy compartment, characteristic deposits and response to immunotherapy. Typical immune-mediated myositis produces symmetric proximal weakness over weeks to months, CK elevation, MRI edema-type signal, myopathic EMG and often inflammatory autoantibodies.

Polymyositis · immune injury of the fiber

Endomysial lymphocytes surrounding non-necrotic fibers are the key histologic recognition point. Macrophages can invade already necrotic myofibers. The pathology location is central: the inflammation attacks individual fibers within fascicles rather than concentrating around vessels.

Dermatomyositis · rash + small edge-of-fascicle fibers

Characteristic photographs in the slides show a heliotrope rash around eyelids, anterior V-sign, posterior shawl sign, Gottron sign on extensor surfaces and Gottron papules over finger joints. Histology shows perivascular inflammation and perifascicular atrophy; the lecture links DM to malignancy screening (especially older adults) and juvenile calcinosis.

Inclusion body myositis · mixed proximal/distal weakness

Think of an older patient with insidious weakness in quadriceps and finger flexors, possible dysphagia, and an ineffective response to routine steroid therapy. Rimmed vacuoles in muscle fibers with endomysial inflammation are its signature. The slide displays trichrome and H&E vacuoles and a β-amyloid immunostain.

DON’T MIX THESE UP
PM = endomysial T cells; DM = perifascicular atrophy + perivascular inflammation + skin rash; IBM = rimmed vacuoles + finger-flexor/quadriceps weakness + poor treatment response.

Other lecture-listed inflammatory entities (for familiarity): immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (anti-SRP, anti-HMGCR; often prominent myonecrosis, with statin association for anti-HMGCR) and antisynthetase syndrome (anti-Jo-1; interstitial lung disease, arthritis, Raynaud phenomenon, mechanic hands).

FIRST AID CONNECTION
Dermatomyositis may signal an underlying malignancy, while antisynthetase antibodies may signal interstitial lung disease. These are immediate clinical extensions of the lecture’s disease associations rather than independent topics.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · Biopsy shows perifascicular atrophy; the patient has Gottron papules. Which immune myopathy?

Dermatomyositis. The rash and perifascicular atrophy separate it from polymyositis and inclusion body myositis.

RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · An older patient has finger-flexor and quadriceps weakness, rimmed vacuoles, and little improvement with steroids. Which myopathy?

Inclusion body myositis.

6 · Selective type-II fiber atrophy

Disuse, chronic exogenous corticosteroids, endogenous glucocorticoid excess (Cushing syndrome) and chronic alcohol exposure are the lecture’s examples. Selective type II fast-twitch fiber atrophy is different from immune-mediated myonecrosis: muscle weakness may reflect reduced fiber size without marked inflammatory destruction or very high CK.

EXAM TRAP
The lecture’s final case pairs weakness during steroid therapy + normal CK + selective type-II atrophy. Do not automatically call this relapsed autoimmune myositis solely because the patient previously had myositis.
RETRIEVAL CHECKPOINT · A treated patient develops new weakness with normal CK and type-II fiber atrophy. What is the course-favored explanation?

Steroid myopathy rather than recurrence of active inflammatory myositis.

7 · Neurogenic atrophy, ALS, SMA and diabetic neuropathy

Unlike primary myopathy, denervation removes neural input and produces abrupt angular atrophic fibers. Surviving axons may reinnervate orphaned fibers, creating a same-type fiber-type grouping instead of the normal checkerboard pattern; repeated loss of innervation produces group atrophy. This morphologic sequence is crucial to differentiate neuropathy from dystrophy or myositis.

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

The course emphasizes combined UMN and LMN degeneration. LMN loss causes weakness, atrophy, hypotonia, reduced reflexes and fasciculations. UMN dysfunction causes spasticity, hyperreflexia and an upgoing plantar response. Respiratory muscle involvement is the major life-limiting consequence. The slide depicts anterior horn loss, corticospinal tract loss and skeletal muscle group atrophy.

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA)

Inherited lower motor neuron degeneration, usually due to SMN1 deficiency on chromosome 5q; SMN2 copy number modifies severity. The slide contrasts early severe SMA type 1 (floppy infant, tongue fasciculations, areflexia, feeding and respiratory difficulty), type 2 (unable to stand independently) and milder later-onset types 3/4. The course explicitly notes that new antisense and gene therapies have improved prognosis.

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy

Diabetes most commonly causes a distal symmetric sensorimotor polyneuropathy, involving axons rather than primary muscle fibers. The course combines metabolic mechanisms (advanced glycation end products and increased sorbitol/NADPH depletion) with ischemic microvascular/vascular injury. The lecture also lists alcohol, heavy metals, solvents, chemotherapy, vasculitis and Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease in the peripheral neuropathy differential.

Fiber-type grouping: motor units are redistributed after surviving axons sprout to reinnervate fibers.

FOUNDATIONS LINK
Apply the chain cell component affected → physiological function lost → characteristic biopsy → clinical phenotype. Structural membrane injury elevates CK; disturbed mitochondrial ATP limits exercise endurance; denervation rearranges motor units; NMJ dysfunction can cause fatigability without primary myofiber degeneration.

MASTER THESE BEFORE MOVING ON

  1. Localization: distinguish myofiber, neuron/axon and NMJ disease before naming the disorder.
  2. Core biopsy vocabulary: round/internal-nucleus fibers versus angular/grouped fibers; necrosis versus regeneration.
  3. Dystrophin mechanism: membrane instability → Ca²⁺ influx → necrosis → fibrofatty replacement; DMD absent versus BMD reduced protein.
  4. Immune myopathy recognition: PM endomysial, DM perifascicular with rash, IBM rimmed vacuoles with distal finger-flexor weakness.
  5. Pattern recognition: ragged red, type-II atrophy and reinnervation grouping belong to different diagnostic classes.

THE MOST USEFUL FIRST AID CONNECTIONS

  1. Gower maneuver + high CK + dystrophin deficit → dystrophinopathy; later cardiopulmonary consequences.
  2. Ragged-red fibers + lactic acidosis → defective mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation.
  3. Gottron/heliotrope + perifascicular atrophy → dermatomyositis; assess malignancy risk.
  4. IBM rimmed vacuoles + finger-flexor weakness → limited response to standard immunosuppression.
  5. SMN1 loss with infantile LMN weakness → SMA; SMN2 modifies phenotype.
